What if the library
was the instrument?

Silverplatter Audio has 90 sound effects packs. Nearly 12,000 sounds. I couldn't stop thinking about what else they could become.

What would happen if that entire collection became the engine of a synthesizer? A recording would no longer be the end of a sound. It would be the beginning of one.

That question became SPASynth. And it took me, a product designer, into the deep end of building my first software instrument.

Oscillator A

A sound becomes
something you play.

The sample engine carries the original idea: bring recorded sounds into an instrument, then give them a musical life beyond the recording.

The design challenge starts here. A library invites you to find a sound. A synth invites you to change it. SPASynth has to make those two ways of thinking belong together.

Organic Chaos

Leave room
for a surprise.

The source material already has character. Organic Chaos brings another question into the interface: how much unpredictability do you want to invite in?

Depth, rate and mix turn that idea into controls. For me, this is where product design becomes especially interesting: making an unfamiliar possibility feel like something you can explore.

The control should invite an experiment.

Modulation matrix

The interesting part
is the connection.

An oscillator, an envelope, a filter. Each has a job. The modulation matrix is where separate parts become a system, connecting a source of movement to a parameter.

That is also the bigger design task. The interface cannot just make each module understandable. It has to help someone understand what those modules can do together.

Then it had
to be real.

Designing the surface was only part of it. This was my first software instrument, built to work both inside a DAW and on its own.

Claude Code and Codex helped me move from a product idea into native audio software. I was learning the instrument by building the instrument.
